    Switches needed

    Reserved by class or actual draw

    A device tells the switch its PoE class when it plugs in. Some switches then reserve the full class amount from the budget, for example 30 W for a class 4 camera that only uses 13 W. Others count what each port actually draws. Check your switch's manual or PoE status page to see which it does, and pick the matching option under Switch.

    Reserving by class is the safe way to plan, because a switch that works this way refuses power to a port once its reservations reach the budget, even when the real draw is low. Many switches can lower a port's reservation through LLDP power negotiation with the device, or let you set a per-port limit.

    Cable length and power loss

    • Keep each run within 100 m (328 ft) of cable, patch cords included. That is the Ethernet limit, with or without PoE.
    • The "device can use" figures already allow for the power lost in a worst-case 100 m run, so do not subtract cable loss again. That gap is why an 802.3af port gives 15.4 W but a device can count on only 12.95 W.
    • The switch measures power at its own port, which includes what the cable loses. The headroom left under your limit covers that, plus IR and heaters at night and PTZ movement.
    • Use solid copper Cat5e or better. Copper-clad aluminium cable runs hotter and loses more power, and is a poor fit for 802.3bt. For runs over 100 m, use a PoE extender or fibre.
